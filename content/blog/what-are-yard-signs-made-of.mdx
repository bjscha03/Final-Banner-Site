Most temporary **yard signs are made of corrugated plastic**: two thin plastic faces joined by a row of hollow channels called flutes. The structure resembles corrugated cardboard, but the faces and internal ribs are plastic rather than paper. It creates a lightweight, rigid panel that can be printed, cut, carried in quantity, and installed on a compatible wire step stake.

That short answer explains the material name. It does not explain whether a specific sign will fit your job. Flute direction, finished size, printed sides, wind exposure, placement rules, stake compatibility, storage, and local recycling access all matter after the material is chosen.

Banners On The Fly currently offers one online yard-sign format: a **24×18-inch corrugated-plastic panel**, single- or double-sided, in supported quantities beginning at ten, with optional step stakes. This guide explains how that physical product behaves so you can decide whether it matches the display you are planning.

## Corrugated-Plastic Yard Sign Anatomy

A finished sign looks like one flat sheet from the front. Viewed from a cut edge, it has three working parts:

1. **Front face:** the printable outer skin on one side;
2. **Fluted core:** parallel plastic ribs and hollow channels through the middle;
3. **Back face:** the second outer skin, which may be blank or printed.

Coroplast, a major manufacturer of corrugated polypropylene sheet, describes its print product as two printable outside facings around an inside core with specifically arranged flutes. Its current [Coroplast Print technical sheet](https://www.coroplast.com/wp-content/uploads/Coroplast-Print-7-2020.pdf) also lists real-estate signs, political signs, construction signage, retail displays, and exhibits among the material's applications.

“Coroplast” is a trademarked product name, although people often use it informally for the broader category. **Corrugated plastic** is the accurate generic term when the exact sheet manufacturer has not been identified.

| Part | What it does | What the buyer should notice |
|---|---|---|
| Printable face | Carries ink and gives the sign its smooth viewing surface | Keep the face clean and protect it from abrasion during transport |
| Internal flutes | Add rigidity without making the panel solid and heavy | Their direction determines how a conventional wire stake enters the sheet |
| Cut edge | Reveals the open channel structure | Inspect for crushing, tearing, or a stake forced through the wrong path |
| Second face | Supports the panel and may carry another printed message | Decide whether a useful second approach exists before paying for two-sided printing |

## Why the Flutes Matter More Than They Look

The flutes are not decorative texture. They are the structure that lets a thin sheet span 24 inches while remaining light enough to handle and stake.

### Flutes create stiffness in one direction

Parallel ribs make the sheet behave differently along and across the channels. A clean panel feels surprisingly rigid for its weight, but it is not a solid board. Crushing an edge, folding the panel sharply, or forcing hardware through an internal rib can permanently damage that structure.

Carry finished signs by supporting the panel rather than pinching one corner under a heavy stack. Keep the edges away from vehicle doors, metal shelving, and other places where the channels can be flattened.

### Flute direction controls stake installation

A conventional wire step stake uses two upper prongs that slide into open channels along the bottom edge. Those channels must run vertically in the installed orientation. If they run horizontally, the prongs meet solid internal ribs instead of open paths.

Do not rotate a finished landscape sign into portrait orientation in the field and assume the same stake will work. The artwork may turn correctly while the internal channels do not. The [yard-sign stake installation guide](/blog/how-to-install-yard-sign-stakes) shows how to identify the bottom edge, align both prongs, and seat the panel without crushing it.

### The edge is useful evidence

Before deploying a stack, inspect one edge in good light. You should see straight, repeated channels rather than crushed cavities, ragged cuts, or buckled faces. Then test one sign with the actual stake on a clean protected surface. A five-minute assembly check is safer than discovering a mismatch at every location on installation day.

## Corrugated Plastic vs. Other Sign Materials

Not every rigid-looking sign is corrugated plastic. Choosing by appearance alone can lead to the wrong installation or storage plan.

| Material category | Basic construction | Practical strength | Main limitation for a typical lawn placement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corrugated plastic | Two plastic faces with hollow parallel flutes | Light, rigid for its weight, printable, compatible with flute-inserted stakes | Edges can crush; the panel is still vulnerable to bending, wind, and poor installation |
| Corrugated cardboard | Paper faces and paper fluting | Inexpensive for protected indoor use | Moisture and outdoor exposure can quickly compromise the paper structure |
| Foam board | Paper or plastic faces around a foam core | Smooth, lightweight display surface | Standard versions are commonly intended for indoor presentations, not direct ground staking |
| Solid plastic sheet | One continuous plastic panel | No hollow channels; can be durable in a suitable thickness | A conventional flute-inserted step stake has nowhere to enter |
| Aluminum composite or metal | Rigid metal faces or solid metal construction | Substantial, long-term sign applications | Heavier, more expensive, and normally requires a different mounting system |

This comparison is about construction, not a universal durability ranking. Material thickness, formulation, ink, edge finishing, support, climate, placement, and duration can change how any sign performs. Buy the complete display system—not a material name in isolation.

## Are Yard Signs Waterproof or Weatherproof?

The plastic sheet does not absorb water like paper corrugate, which is one reason it is used for short-term outdoor signs. That does **not** make a printed yard sign indestructible or suitable for every weather condition.

Outdoor performance still depends on:

- the exact sheet and ink system;
- ultraviolet exposure;
- temperature and repeated expansion or contraction;
- wind acting on the broad printed face;
- soil stability and stake depth;
- impacts from people, equipment, landscaping, or vehicles;
- how often the sign is inspected, cleaned, moved, and stored.

Coroplast's product FAQ notes that outdoor life varies with geography and weather, and it reports a specific laboratory exposure result for one regular white 4 mm product. That manufacturer-specific test is useful context, but it is **not a lifetime promise for every printed sign**. Ink, fabrication, location, installation, and actual weather are separate variables.

Treat “weather-resistant” as a planning characteristic, not permission to leave a lightweight sign out through severe conditions. The National Weather Service advises postponing outdoor activities when a wind advisory or high-wind warning is in effect. If conditions can move the panel, bend the stake, or turn the sign into debris, remove it and reinstall only after the location is safe.

## How Printing Changes the Material Decision

The sheet may be simple, but the useful sign depends on what is printed on it.

### Single-sided printing

One printed face makes sense when viewers approach from one controlled direction or when the back sits against landscaping, a wall, or another visual barrier. Paying for a second face that nobody can see does not improve the message.

### Double-sided printing

Two printed faces can serve an open lawn, opposing foot traffic, or vehicles approaching from two directions. The two sides do not have to carry identical information. Directional arrows, arrival and exit messages, or front/back event instructions may need deliberate differences.

The [single-sided versus double-sided yard-sign guide](/blog/single-vs-double-sided-yard-signs) explains how to map actual approaches before choosing. Preview both faces, especially when arrows or dates change.

### Surface area is limited

A 24×18 panel gives one short message room to work. Corrugated plastic does not compensate for a crowded layout. A small phone number, long URL, detailed service list, disclaimer, portrait, QR code, and large headline cannot all be the first priority.

Use the [standard yard-sign size guide](/blog/standard-yard-sign-size-guide) to decide whether the supported panel fits the site. Then apply the hierarchy and distance-testing process in the [banner readability guide](/blog/banner-design-readability-tips). The material can hold excellent print; the viewer still needs enough time and contrast to understand it.

## Match the Sign to the Actual Use

Corrugated-plastic yard signs work best when the message, placement, and hardware all suit a temporary rigid panel.

### Strong fits

- open-house identification and approved directional routes;
- campaign or issue signs where the design follows applicable rules;
- event parking, check-in, pickup, and wayfinding;
- contractor identification on a property with permission;
- school, church, fundraiser, and community-event messaging;
- retail or service promotions in an approved landscaped area.

### Situations that need another plan

- a hard surface with nowhere for a ground stake;
- a very large message intended to span a fence or storefront;
- a permanent sign subject to building, electrical, structural, or sign-code requirements;
- a location with no property permission;
- an exposed site where a lightweight staked panel cannot be managed safely;
- custom dimensions outside the current online 24×18 product.

## A Pre-Order Material and Site Checklist

Use this workflow before uploading artwork:

### 1. Confirm the approved location

Identify the property owner, site manager, event organizer, or authority that controls the placement. Check applicable sign rules, right-of-way boundaries, sightlines, accessibility, installation dates, and removal deadlines.

### 2. Map every viewing direction

Stand where the viewer will be. Note approach direction, distance, speed, landscaping, parked vehicles, shadows, and competing signs. Decide whether one or two printed faces will serve those approaches.

### 3. Confirm the ground and utilities

Step stakes are for compatible soil—not concrete, asphalt, gravel fill, pavers, or an improvised bucket. Check for irrigation, landscape wiring, roots, underground utilities, and other obstructions before inserting anything into the ground.

### 4. Count panels, designs, and stakes separately

Ten signs do not always require ten stakes. Some panels may be displayed in windows, frames, or other approved systems. Conversely, a route may need more staked placements than the first artwork count suggests. Build a location schedule before checkout.

### 5. Test the finished design at size

Print a reduced proof, view the design from across a room, or place it on a screen at a representative visual angle. Check the main message first, then the action. If viewers cannot identify both quickly, edit the copy instead of relying on the material to create visibility.

### 6. Plan removal, cleaning, and storage

Assign a person and date for retrieval. Wipe dry soil from stakes, let damp panels dry completely, and store the signs flat or upright with full support. Do not trap grit between printed faces or lean a tall stack until it bows.

## Can Corrugated-Plastic Yard Signs Be Reused?

Often, yes—if the message remains accurate and the panel, print, and edges are still in good condition. Reuse is easiest when the artwork avoids dates, temporary prices, or names that change every season.

Before reinstalling a stored sign, check:

- both faces for fading, scratches, stains, or outdated information;
- every edge for crushed flutes or splits;
- the panel for a permanent curve or crease;
- the stake for bent prongs, damaged welds, rust, or sharp burrs;
- the new site for permission, soil compatibility, and updated rules.

Do not cover an obsolete message with a small patch that creates ambiguity. A clean reprint is usually better than a directional sign whose old arrow, date, or destination can still be seen.

## Can Yard Signs Be Recycled?

The base sheet is commonly polypropylene, and the manufacturer states that standard Coroplast sheets can generally be recycled after non-polypropylene accessories are removed. **Local acceptance is the deciding factor.** A curbside program that accepts polypropylene bottles may not accept printed corrugated sheet, large rigid panels, or commercial quantities.

The Federal Trade Commission's [consumer guidance on recyclable claims](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/eco-friendly-and-green-marketing-claims) makes the same practical distinction: a material described as recyclable may not be accepted where you live. Ask the city, county, waste hauler, or drop-off facility about printed corrugated polypropylene specifically.

Before recycling or disposal:

1. Remove wire stakes, fasteners, adhesive accessories, and unrelated materials.
2. Keep reusable stakes for the next compatible sign.
3. Ask whether the facility accepts printed corrugated polypropylene sheet and what sizes it permits.
4. Follow its preparation rules; do not assume a resin number guarantees curbside acceptance.
5. If recycling is unavailable, consider legitimate reuse before disposal, but do not create a new safety or litter problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the plastic in yard signs called?

The generic material is **corrugated plastic**, usually a fluted polypropylene sheet. Coroplast is a well-known trademarked brand name that is often used conversationally for this product category.

### Are corrugated-plastic signs the same as cardboard?

No. The internal fluted structure is similar in concept, but corrugated cardboard uses paper faces and a paper core. Corrugated-plastic signs use plastic faces and ribs, so they respond differently to moisture, printing, handling, and recycling.

### Why are there holes in the edge of a yard sign?

Those openings are the ends of the internal flutes. They reduce weight and create stiffness. When the channels run vertically, compatible wire-stake prongs can slide into selected flutes from the bottom edge.

### Can a corrugated-plastic sign be printed on both sides?

Yes. Banners On The Fly currently offers single- and double-sided printing for its 24×18 yard sign. Choose two sides only when both faces serve a real viewing direction, and verify directional arrows independently.

### Can I put a yard sign stake through any edge?

No. The prongs need to enter open channels aligned with the installed orientation. Forcing a stake through internal ribs can crush the panel or bend the hardware. Use the intended bottom edge and a compatible stake.

### How long will a corrugated-plastic yard sign last outside?

There is no responsible universal number. The sheet formulation, ink, sunlight, wind, temperature, installation, impacts, and maintenance all affect life. Inspect the sign regularly and remove it when the print, panel, stake, location, or weather no longer supports safe use.

## Choose the Material After You Choose the Job

Corrugated plastic earns its place in yard signage because the fluted structure provides a useful balance of printable area, stiffness, low weight, and stake compatibility. Its limits are just as important: it is a temporary lightweight panel, not a structural or permanent sign.

Start with the approved location and viewing direction. Then decide on printed sides, count the placements, confirm the ground, simplify the artwork, and plan retrieval.
