# Political Sign Disclaimer Requirements: A Pre-Print Checklist

For a federal campaign, a political sign disclaimer is not a line to add after the design is finished. Determine **who paid for the sign, whether a candidate authorized it, which rules apply, and the exact approved wording before the layout is built**. Then place that statement in a readable, contrasting box and preserve it through every size and side of the finished sign.

The federal rule is not the only possible requirement. State campaign-finance law, local temporary-sign ordinances, highway and right-of-way rules, polling-place restrictions, property agreements, and an organization's own approval process can all affect a sign. This guide explains the current federal starting point and a practical artwork workflow; it is not legal advice. For a real campaign, confirm the final language and placement with the campaign's treasurer, counsel, relevant election agency, and local authority before printing.

The timing matters. On July 6, 2026, the Federal Election Commission reminded committees that covered public communications include yard signs and billboards. The current [FEC advertising and disclaimer guidance](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/advertising-and-disclaimers/) and [11 CFR 110.11](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-11/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-110/section-110.11) should be the source documents for a federal sign—not an old campaign file or a competitor's template.

## The short federal answer

Under 11 CFR 110.11, all public communications made by a political committee must carry the required disclaimer. The rule also covers specified public communications by other people, including communications that expressly advocate the election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate, solicit contributions, or qualify as electioneering communications.

The required content changes with the relationship between the payer and candidate:

| Who paid for the communication? | Was it authorized by a candidate or candidate committee? | Federal content starting point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Candidate or authorized candidate committee | Yes | Identify the authorized political committee that paid for it |
| Another person or group | Yes | Identify the payer and state that the candidate or campaign authorized it |
| Another person or group | No | Identify the payer, provide the required contact information, and state that no candidate or candidate committee authorized it |

Do not treat this table as fill-in-the-blank legal language. Committee type, payer identity, authorization, election level, and the nature of the communication matter. The FEC publishes a specific [candidate committee yard-sign example](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/making-disbursements/advertising/candidate-committee-yard-sign-example/) and separate examples for independent expenditures and party committees.

### What the federal printed-disclaimer rules require

When 11 CFR 110.11 requires a disclaimer on a printed public communication, the rule says it must be clear and conspicuous. It also sets these presentation requirements:

- The type must be large enough to be clearly readable.
- The disclaimer must sit inside a printed box separated from the rest of the communication.
- The statement needs reasonable color contrast with its background.
- Black text on white is one federal contrast safe harbor; equivalent contrast relative to the largest text is another.
- For a sign no larger than 24×36 inches, 12-point type satisfies the federal type-size safe harbor.

That 12-point provision is a legal safe harbor for certain federal printed communications, not a design recommendation for every setting. Twelve-point copy can be technically present yet difficult to inspect on a sign planted several feet from the viewer. Build a disclaimer that remains comfortably readable on the actual output, not merely visible at 800% zoom in a design program.

### Small-item exceptions are narrow

Federal regulations list examples such as pens, pins, buttons, and bumper stickers where a disclaimer cannot be conveniently printed, plus media where display would be impracticable. The FEC's July 2026 reminder specifically names yard signs among covered communications. Do not assume a standard yard sign qualifies for the small-item exception.

## Federal, state, and local rules answer different questions

Federal guidance explains federal campaign-finance disclaimer obligations. It does not create one nationwide placement rule for every lawn, road, apartment community, or polling place.

Use three separate review tracks:

| Review track | Questions to resolve | Best source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal campaign finance | Is the communication covered? Who paid? Was it authorized? What content and presentation are required? | FEC guidance, current eCFR, committee treasurer or counsel |
| State election and campaign finance | Is an attribution required? Is additional wording, size, placement, printer identification, or retention information required? | Official state election or campaign-finance agency and current state law |
| Local placement and property | Where, when, and for how long may the sign be installed? Are permits, setbacks, size limits, or removal dates involved? | City or county code office, transportation authority, property owner, landlord, or association |

Rules can differ materially from one jurisdiction to another. A state may treat a yard sign differently from a building-mounted banner or a vehicle magnet. A city may regulate temporary-sign dimensions and public rights-of-way even when the message itself is political. A polling place can have separate electioneering boundaries. Property permission remains important even when the sign's content is protected speech.

The practical rule is simple: **verify the precise sign, payer, election, location, and installation date—not merely the state name.** Save the official source, contact name, and approval date in the job folder.

## A political sign disclaimer workflow that prevents reprints

### 1. Identify the actual payer

Start with the entity whose funds will pay the printing invoice. Record the entity's full legal or registered name and any committee name used in its filings. Do not infer the payer from the candidate name on the front of the sign.

Ask:

- Is the payer the candidate's authorized committee?
- Is a party committee, PAC, nonprofit, company, labor organization, or individual paying?
- Are costs shared across committees or candidates?
- Does the printed piece also solicit a contribution?
- Will the same artwork be used for a yard sign, banner, handout, vehicle sign, or digital ad?

One design file can turn into several legally distinct communications. A statement approved for one payer or format should not automatically be copied to another.

### 2. Document authorization

Authorization changes the required federal content. Obtain the determination from the campaign's compliance decision-maker and record it in writing. A designer or printer should not make this call from an email signature, logo package, or casual statement that the candidate “knows about” the project.

Use a short job record:

- Paying entity
- Candidate and office
- Authorized: yes, no, or pending review
- Reviewer and role
- Approved disclaimer text
- Approval date
- Source or citation used
- Formats and quantities covered by the approval

If authorization is unresolved, hold the artwork. Redesigning a footer after proofs have circulated is faster than correcting thousands of printed signs.

### 3. Get exact wording approved

Copy the complete, approved disclaimer from the compliance reviewer. Preserve capitalization, punctuation, committee name, web address, phone number, or street address exactly as approved.

Do not shorten an entity name merely to fit the layout. Do not replace required text with a logo. Do not turn “not authorized” language into a symbol or footnote. If the statement is too long, revise the sign hierarchy or choose a format that can carry it clearly.

### 4. Reserve the disclaimer box before styling the sign

Treat the disclaimer as a fixed content region. On a standard horizontal yard sign, a bottom band often works because it creates a consistent edge and stays separate from the candidate name. On a tall banner, a footer or side panel may be more appropriate. The correct location depends on the entire communication and applicable rules.

Build the disclaimer region first, then design the candidate name, office, slogan, and supporting details around it. This prevents the common failure where a beautiful campaign layout reaches final review with no credible space left for compliance text.

### 5. Test actual output size

The file's zoom percentage does not establish printed readability. Export a proof at the final dimensions and check:

- Final type size, not the type size of a reduced-scale document
- Box rule thickness after scaling
- Contrast in the exported file
- Adequate clearance from trim, fold, hem, or stake flutes
- No logo, photo, pattern, or gradient passing behind the statement
- No cropping in the printer's on-screen preview

For Banners On The Fly's yard signs, the supported product is a fixed **24×18-inch corrugated-plastic sign**, ordered in quantities of 10 with single- or double-sided printing and optional step stakes. That format is within the federal 24×36-inch printed safe-harbor dimension, but the campaign still owns the duty to supply correct, readable, legally approved artwork.

### 6. Review every printed side and format

A double-sided yard sign is not finished because the front looks correct. Inspect both sides independently. Confirm the artwork orientation, disclaimer, candidate name, office, arrows, dates, and color on each printed face.

If campaign counsel has approved identical disclaimer treatment on both sides, repeat it consistently. If different communications or sponsors appear on different sides, stop and obtain format-specific direction. Do not assume a statement on one face cures a missing or incorrect statement elsewhere.

The same caution applies when adapting the design to a [political vinyl banner](/political-signs), car magnet, poster, or social graphic. Each output has different space, visibility, and potentially different legal treatment.

### 7. Lock compliance elements before production

After approval, lock the disclaimer group in the artwork. Name the final file clearly and archive the approved proof.

A useful filename pattern is:

`candidate-office-format-side-version-approved-date.pdf`

Keep a separate editable master. Production should receive only the approved export, not a folder containing “final,” “final-2,” and “really-final” versions with no record of which was cleared.

## Design the sign to be read, not merely noticed

Compliance does not rescue an unreadable campaign sign. A successful 24×18 yard sign normally needs a short hierarchy:

1. Candidate surname or campaign name
2. Office sought
3. One optional short supporting cue
4. Required disclaimer

The name should dominate. The office needs enough contrast and size to clarify the race. Supporting text should earn its space. Phone numbers, long slogans, multiple endorsements, portraits, QR codes, social handles, and dates compete with the information a moving viewer can actually absorb.

Use the [banner readability guide](/blog/banner-design-readability-tips) to test hierarchy and contrast, then apply the same distance-first thinking to yard signs. Print the layout on ordinary paper at a reduced but known scale, step back, and ask someone unfamiliar with the campaign what they can identify immediately.

### Keep the disclaimer separate without hiding it

A disclaimer box can be visually quiet without being obscure. Use a plain background, solid border, consistent padding, and a typeface with open letterforms. Avoid:

- Ultra-condensed legal copy
- Hairline type or borders
- Low-contrast gray over a photograph
- A patterned or gradient background
- Text placed over corrugation shadows in a mockup
- A footer cropped by the final trim
- A disclaimer disguised as decorative microcopy

The [print-ready artwork checklist](/blog/print-ready-banner-artwork-guide) covers font embedding, raster quality, and final-file review. Keep the disclaimer as vector text whenever possible so it remains sharp.

## Single-sided or double-sided political yard signs?

The right choice depends on traffic flow and placement, not a belief that two sides are always better.

| Placement | Likely useful format | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lawn facing one primary direction | Single-sided may be sufficient | Only one approach has a useful sight line |
| Perpendicular to two-way pedestrian or vehicle traffic | Double-sided may improve visibility | Each approach sees a printed face |
| Window or wall-mounted display | Single-sided often fits | The back is not visible |
| Open corner with several approaches | Review the exact angle first | Two faces help only if the sign orientation supports them |

Read the full [single-sided versus double-sided yard sign guide](/blog/single-vs-double-sided-yard-signs) before ordering. Whichever format you choose, confirm the compliance treatment for every visible printed face.

## Pre-print political sign checklist

### Compliance approval

- [ ] We identified the exact payer.
- [ ] We documented whether the communication is authorized.
- [ ] We determined which federal rules apply.
- [ ] We checked current official state requirements.
- [ ] We checked location-specific temporary-sign, right-of-way, and electioneering rules.
- [ ] The treasurer, counsel, or designated compliance reviewer approved the exact wording.

### Artwork

- [ ] The disclaimer is inside a clearly separated printed box where required.
- [ ] Type is readable at final output size.
- [ ] Text and background have strong contrast.
- [ ] The box is clear of trim, folds, hems, holes, and stake flutes.
- [ ] Candidate name, office, and disclaimer are spelled correctly.
- [ ] Fonts are embedded or converted appropriately in the production PDF.
- [ ] Every side and format received its own review.

### Placement and operations

- [ ] Property permission is documented.
- [ ] The sign does not block a sidewalk, accessible route, driveway sight line, or traffic control device.
- [ ] Installation and removal dates are assigned.
- [ ] Volunteers received approved placement instructions.
- [ ] The campaign has a collection, reuse, recycling, or disposal plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all political yard signs need a “paid for by” disclaimer?

No single sentence answers every sign. For federal elections, 11 CFR 110.11 specifies which public communications require disclaimers and what they must contain. Political-committee public communications are covered, while the rule has defined scope and exceptions for other speakers and media. State and local rules can add or differ. Verify the payer, authorization, election, format, and jurisdiction with the appropriate official source and compliance reviewer.

### What should a federal candidate committee's yard-sign disclaimer say?

The federal rule requires a covered communication paid for and authorized by the candidate or authorized committee to identify the authorized political committee that paid. Use the committee's approved exact language and review the FEC's official candidate-committee yard-sign example; do not substitute a generic template.

### Does the disclaimer need a box?

For a printed public communication covered by the federal rule, 11 CFR 110.11 requires the disclaimer to be in a printed box set apart from the other content. Additional state requirements may also apply.

### Is 12-point disclaimer text always enough?

No. Under the federal rule, 12-point type is a size safe harbor for signs and other listed printed materials no larger than 24×36 inches. The disclaimer must still be clear, conspicuous, and reasonably contrasting, and other laws may require different treatment. Larger communications are evaluated differently.

### Should a double-sided political sign have the disclaimer on both sides?

Have the campaign's compliance reviewer approve the treatment for the complete two-sided communication. Operationally, inspect and approve both printed faces rather than assuming one approved side automatically covers the other.

### Can the printer write or approve the legal disclaimer?

The campaign or sponsoring organization should provide and approve the exact required language. A printer can preserve supplied text, check production legibility, and flag obvious artwork risks, but should not determine payer status, authorization, or legal compliance for the campaign.
