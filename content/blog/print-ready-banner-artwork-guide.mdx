The best **print-ready banner artwork** is not necessarily the file with the most layers, the largest megabyte count, or the highest possible resolution. It is the file that matches the banner's proportions, keeps important content clear of finished edges and hardware, preserves enough detail at the final size, and opens predictably when it reaches production.

If you need the short version, use this six-point test before uploading:

1. Build the artwork in the exact proportion of the banner you plan to order.
2. Use vector artwork for logos and text whenever possible.
3. Check raster images at their effective resolution at final print size.
4. Embed fonts or convert final text to outlines in the exported copy.
5. Keep essential words, logos, and QR codes comfortably inside the finished edge.
6. Export a clean PDF, PNG, JPG, or JPEG and inspect the exported file—not only the working document.

Banners On The Fly currently accepts **PDF, PNG, JPG, and JPEG files up to 50MB** in the online design tool. PDF is often the strongest choice for layouts containing vector logos and type, while a properly sized PNG or JPG can work well for raster-based designs. The guide below explains when to use each format and how to avoid the problems that most often make large-format artwork look soft, stretched, crowded, or unexpectedly different from the screen.

## Start With the Finished Banner Size and Proportion

Artwork setup begins with the physical display, not the export dialog. Measure the installation area, choose the banner's finished dimensions, and then create a document with the same width-to-height ratio.

A 3 × 6-foot banner has a 1:2 ratio. A 4 × 8-foot banner has that same ratio, so the same layout can scale proportionally between them. A 3 × 8-foot banner has a different ratio. Placing 1:2 artwork into that wider shape requires one of three compromises: cropping, empty space, or stretching.

Stretching is usually the worst choice because circles become ovals, logos lose their intended shape, and people or products look distorted. Cropping can be acceptable when the design has generous background space, but it becomes risky when text or logos sit close to the edges.

If you have not settled on dimensions yet, use the [banner size guide](/blog/perfect-banner-size-guide) before building the artwork. Size, viewing distance, mounting location, and layout should be decided together.

### Full-size setup versus scaled setup

You can build at full size or at an exact scale such as 1:2 or 1:10. Full-size setup is simpler because every measurement corresponds directly to the finished banner. Scaled setup can make very large Illustrator or Photoshop documents easier to manage, but the scale must be communicated and applied consistently.

For example, artwork for a 48 × 96-inch banner created at 1:2 scale should use a 24 × 48-inch artboard. All raster images in that half-scale document need twice the pixel density required at final size because they will be enlarged 200% for output.

| Finished banner | Document at full size | Document at 1:2 scale | Ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 × 6 ft | 36 × 72 in | 18 × 36 in | 1:2 |
| 4 × 8 ft | 48 × 96 in | 24 × 48 in | 1:2 |
| 3 × 8 ft | 36 × 96 in | 18 × 48 in | 3:8 |
| 4 × 10 ft | 48 × 120 in | 24 × 60 in | 2:5 |

Use one scale throughout the file. Mixing a half-scale artboard with measurements or images prepared for a different scale is a common source of accidental softness.

## Understand Vector Artwork and Raster Images

Large-format designs usually contain both vector and raster elements. Knowing the difference makes resolution decisions much easier.

### Vector graphics

Vector artwork describes shapes mathematically. Logos, icons, borders, and live type created in Illustrator or another vector application can scale without becoming pixelated. That makes vectors ideal for the elements viewers must read clearly.

A logo placed from a genuine SVG, EPS, AI, or vector PDF is usually more dependable than the same logo copied from a website. A web logo may be only a few hundred pixels wide even if it looks sharp on a laptop.

### Raster images

Photos, screenshots, textures, and most PNG or JPG files are made from pixels. Enlarging a raster image spreads the same pixels over a larger area. Once the image no longer contains enough detail for its printed size and viewing distance, edges become blocky or soft.

The important number is **effective PPI at final size**, not the PPI typed into the file metadata. Changing a low-resolution image from 72 PPI to 300 PPI without adding meaningful image data does not restore lost detail. Adobe likewise notes that undersized raster images can pixelate, while excessive resolution can enlarge files without improving visible output ([Adobe's raster and vector overview](https://helpx.adobe.com/photoshop/using/image-essentials.html)).

## Choose a Practical Resolution at Final Size

For many large-format banner designs, **100 PPI at final size is a practical minimum target**, especially when the banner is viewed from several feet away. For photography, detailed illustrations, or signs viewed more closely, working toward **150 PPI at final size** can provide additional detail when the source image supports it.

Roland DG's large-format file-preparation guidance uses at least 100 dpi at 100% size as a working industry standard and explains that a half-size layout should carry twice that resolution before enlargement ([Roland DG large-format preparation guide](https://www.rolanddg.eu/en/blog/preparing-files-for-large-format-print)). This is a preparation guideline, not a promise that every image at 100 PPI will look equally sharp. Viewing distance, source quality, compression, subject matter, and print process all matter.

Use this formula:

**Required pixels = finished dimension in inches × target PPI**

| Finished size | Pixels at 100 PPI | Pixels at 150 PPI |
|---|---:|---:|
| 3 × 6 ft | 3,600 × 7,200 | 5,400 × 10,800 |
| 4 × 8 ft | 4,800 × 9,600 | 7,200 × 14,400 |
| 3 × 10 ft | 3,600 × 12,000 | 5,400 × 18,000 |
| 4 × 12 ft | 4,800 × 14,400 | 7,200 × 21,600 |

Do not assume a 300-PPI full-size file is automatically better. On a 4 × 12-foot banner, that would require 14,400 × 43,200 pixels before considering layers, which can create an enormous document. Large-format artwork should be detailed enough for the intended viewing distance without becoming unnecessarily difficult to save, upload, or process.

### A quick image-quality check

Open each important raster image at 100% zoom and inspect faces, product edges, small logos, and areas with fine contrast. Then check the image's pixel dimensions against the size it occupies on the finished banner.

Also ask where the audience will stand. A texture that looks slightly soft from 12 inches away may look completely natural on a banner read from across a room or street. Critical text and logos should still be vector whenever possible.

## Pick the Right File Format

Banners On The Fly's current upload flow accepts PDF, PNG, JPG, and JPEG files with a maximum file size of 50MB. Each format has a useful role.

### PDF: best for mixed vector and raster layouts

Choose PDF when the design contains logos, type, vector shapes, and placed photography. A correctly exported PDF can preserve vectors, embed fonts, carry color-profile information, and keep the complete layout in one file.

Adobe's current print-ready workflow recommends PDF/X-4 when converting a standard PDF for professional printing, because the standard is designed to preserve the information needed for dependable output ([Adobe's print-ready PDF guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/acrobat/desktop/print-documents/set-up-and-print-pdfs/print-ready.html)). You do not need to force every banner into PDF/X without understanding the settings, but a press-quality or PDF/X export is generally more suitable than a screen-optimized PDF.

### PNG: useful for crisp raster graphics

PNG works well for flat graphics, screenshots, illustrations, or designs requiring lossless raster export. It can preserve transparency, although you should confirm how transparent areas appear against the actual banner background in the preview.

PNG does not magically make a small source image print-ready. The exported pixel dimensions still must support the final printed size.

### JPG or JPEG: useful for photographic artwork

JPG can be efficient for photo-heavy artwork, but it uses lossy compression. Export once at a high-quality setting from the original document. Repeatedly opening and resaving a JPG can introduce halos, blockiness, and noise around text and sharp edges.

Avoid screenshots of a design, images downloaded from social media, and files sent through apps that automatically compress photos. Return to the original artwork whenever possible.

## Handle Fonts, Links, and Effects Before Export

Fonts and linked images can look perfect on the designer's computer but change when the file is opened elsewhere.

For a final PDF, use one of these approaches:

- Embed fonts during PDF export when the font license permits it.
- Convert text to outlines in a duplicate final-output file.
- Keep the editable source with live type in case wording needs to change later.

Outlining prevents font substitution, but outlined text is harder to edit and may become less accessible inside the source file. That is why the outlined version should be an export copy, not your only working document.

Embed placed logos and images, or package the source file before archiving it. Illustrator's Links panel distinguishes linked assets from embedded assets; once an image is embedded, it becomes part of the document rather than depending on an external file path ([Adobe's linked and embedded file guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/illustrator/using/manage-linked-and-embedded-files.html)).

Finally, inspect drop shadows, transparency, gradients, clipping masks, and overprint settings in the exported file. Complex effects are not necessarily a problem, but they deserve a visual check at high zoom.

## Plan Color for Print, Not Just for One Screen

A monitor creates color with light. A printed banner reflects light from ink and material. Those two systems cannot reproduce every color in exactly the same way, and two uncalibrated displays may not even agree with each other.

Use consistent brand color values, avoid judging color from a phone with automatic brightness or vivid-display settings, and retain an embedded color profile when your software supports it. If exact color is business-critical, keep the original brand specification and discuss the requirement before production rather than relying on a screenshot.

Do not blindly convert a file between RGB and CMYK multiple times. Each conversion uses a profile and rendering method. Adobe's Illustrator documentation explains that the output profile describes the target printing condition and guides how color values are translated ([Adobe PDF color-management options](https://helpx.adobe.com/illustrator/using/pdf-options.html)). A clean, consistently managed file is better than a file that has passed through several unknown conversions.

Expect extremely bright RGB blues, greens, and oranges to appear different in print. Very dark colors with only slight separation can also merge visually from a distance. Strong contrast and simple color relationships usually survive large-format viewing better than subtle screen-only effects.

## Protect Important Content From Edges and Finishing

Background colors and photos intended to reach the edge should extend fully to the artwork boundary. At the same time, essential content needs breathing room.

Keep these items comfortably inside the finished edge:

- Headlines and dates
- Logos and sponsor marks
- Phone numbers and web addresses
- QR codes
- Faces and product details
- Thin borders that would look uneven if shifted slightly

The safe distance depends on banner size, finishing, and installation. A small indoor banner and a large outdoor banner should not use the same visual margin simply because both are measured in inches.

Grommets, pole pockets, hems, and mounting tension all compete with edge space. Use the site's live preview to check the final composition, and leave more room than looks barely sufficient on the screen. ## Design for the Actual Viewing Distance

Technical file quality cannot rescue a layout that asks the audience to read too much.

Start with one primary message, then establish a clear hierarchy:

1. The headline or offer
2. The brand or event name
3. The essential action, date, or location
4. Supporting details only when they remain readable

Zoom out until the design occupies roughly the same visual angle it will have from the expected viewing position. If every line appears equally important, nothing is guiding the reader.

Use strong contrast and avoid placing fine type over a busy photo. QR codes need clean surrounding space and should be tested from an appropriate distance before the file is finalized. Do not make a QR code the only way to communicate essential information.

Material and environment also affect design decisions. Solid [vinyl banners](/vinyl-banners) provide a continuous print surface, while [mesh banners](/mesh-banners) use perforations that can reduce the visual density of fine detail. Compare the materials in the [vinyl-versus-mesh guide](/blog/vinyl-vs-mesh-banners-guide) before preparing an intricate fence graphic.

## Inspect the Exported File Before Uploading

The exported file is the production handoff, so open that exact file in a separate viewer and perform a final review.

### Pre-upload checklist

- [ ] Finished width, height, orientation, and proportion match the order.
- [ ] The file contains only the intended banner page or artboard.
- [ ] Logos and type remain sharp when zoomed in.
- [ ] Raster images have enough effective resolution at final size.
- [ ] Fonts are embedded or outlined in the final export copy.
- [ ] Linked images are embedded or otherwise included.
- [ ] Background artwork reaches every edge where full coverage is intended.
- [ ] Essential content is safely inside the edge and finishing area.
- [ ] No crop marks, registration marks, guides, notes, or template layers appear unless specifically required.
- [ ] Spelling, dates, phone numbers, URLs, and QR codes have been tested.
- [ ] The exported file opens without missing-image or font warnings.
- [ ] The file is PDF, PNG, JPG, or JPEG and no larger than 50MB.
- [ ] The live preview shows the intended crop, scale, and position.

Do not skip the preview because the source document looked correct. Banners On The Fly's [online design tool](/design) lets you enter the product dimensions, upload artwork, and review its placement before continuing. Treat that preview as the final composition check.

## Common Print-Ready Artwork Mistakes

### Using a website image as a large logo

The logo looks sharp in a browser because it is displayed small. Enlarged across several feet, the same file may show visible pixels. Locate the original vector logo or request it from the person who created the brand assets.

### Designing first and choosing the banner size later

This creates last-minute cropping and awkward empty space. Select the finished dimensions first, then build the layout to the correct proportion.

### Adding a border too close to the edge

Even a small finishing or alignment variation makes a thin border look uneven. Move it inward substantially or remove it.

### Assuming a larger file means a sharper print

File size can come from layers, uncompressed data, or metadata. It does not prove that the source photo contains useful detail. Check pixel dimensions and effective PPI instead.

### Sending the working file without checking the export

Missing links, font substitutions, hidden layers, and incorrect artboards often appear only after export. Review the exact PDF, PNG, or JPG you plan to upload.

### Filling the banner with too much copy

A technically perfect paragraph is still difficult to read from a moving car or a crowded event aisle. Edit the message before shrinking the type.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best file format for printing a vinyl banner?

A print-quality PDF is usually the best choice for a layout combining vector logos, text, shapes, and photos. PNG or high-quality JPG can also print well when their pixel dimensions support the final banner size. Banners On The Fly currently accepts PDF, PNG, JPG, and JPEG.

### Is 300 PPI required for banner artwork?

Not usually at full banner size. Large-format graphics are commonly viewed from farther away than brochures or photographs. A practical target is often 100 PPI at final size, with 150 PPI useful for closer viewing or detailed imagery. Source quality and viewing distance matter as much as the number.

### Can I enlarge a small JPG for a banner?

You can enlarge it, but enlargement cannot recreate detail that was never captured. If the image becomes soft or blocky at its effective printed size, find a higher-resolution original or redesign the layout so the image occupies less space.

### Should banner artwork be RGB or CMYK?

Use a consistent, color-managed workflow and avoid repeated conversions. Many design applications begin in RGB, while professional printing uses device-specific ink color spaces. When you know the correct output profile, follow it; otherwise preserve the best original and export a stable file rather than guessing through multiple conversions.

### Do I need bleed or crop marks?

Extend backgrounds intended for full coverage to the artwork edge, but do not add crop marks or extra page area unless the printer's workflow specifically requests them. More importantly, keep essential content away from edges and finishing hardware.

### Can I upload artwork created in Canva or another online design app?

Yes, provided the exported PDF, PNG, or JPG matches the banner's proportion, retains enough resolution, and passes the final checklist. Choose print-quality export settings when available, then inspect the downloaded file before uploading it.
