# Trade Show Booth Checklist: Plan Banners, Setup, and Backup Gear

A useful **trade show booth checklist** starts with the exhibitor manual, not a shopping list. Confirm the official booth dimensions, display rules, installation method, move-in window, shipping instructions, utilities, and venue restrictions. Then give every banner one specific communication job, build the artwork at the final size, test the complete booth before it ships, and pack the tools and duplicate files needed to recover from predictable problems.

The goal is not to fill every surface. It is to make the booth easy to recognize from the aisle, easy to enter, and easy for staff to operate. A polished 10×10 space can often do that with one strong back-wall message, a clear table or counter zone, and a few intentional supporting elements.

This checklist focuses on vinyl banners and the decisions around them, while accounting for the booth, freight, accessibility, staff, and teardown steps that determine whether the printed graphics actually work.

## The one-page trade show booth checklist

| Stage | Confirm | Deliverable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Show approval | Booth size, orientation, height limits, approved structures, move-in time, labor and utility rules | Annotated exhibitor manual and floor plan |
| Message plan | Audience, primary category, one buyer benefit, next action | One-sentence booth promise and message hierarchy |
| Banner plan | Location, visible dimensions, viewing distance, mounting method, material, finish | Dimensioned graphic schedule |
| Artwork | Final-size document, readable type, vector logo, correct crop, protected finish areas | Approved production PDF and backup export |
| Logistics | Carrier, advance warehouse or direct-to-show address, labels, deadlines, material handling | Shipment record and case inventory |
| Test setup | Complete frame, banner, table, lighting, samples, cables, staff positions | Photos, fixes, and packed hardware by case |
| Move-in | Freight located, banner inspected, structure level, attachments secure, route clear | Show-ready booth and exception list |
| Show open | Message visible, staff sight lines open, supplies hidden, leads tested | Daily opening photo and replenishment plan |
| Move-out | Graphics clean and dry, hardware counted, cases labeled, outbound documents complete | Return-shipment record and condition notes |

Print that table or copy it into the project board. Every line should have one owner and a due date tied to the actual organizer schedule.

## 1. Read the exhibitor manual before designing

The exhibit rules control what will fit and what may be installed. Booth labels such as “10×10” describe a contracted footprint, not necessarily a clear 10-foot wall where any banner can be attached.

Check the current event's official documents for:

- Booth number, dimensions, and whether the space is inline, corner, peninsula, or island
- Back-wall and side-wall height limits
- Line-of-sight rules near the aisle
- Pipe-and-drape dimensions and whether exhibitors may attach graphics to it
- Required or prohibited hanging methods
- Fire-safety and material documentation
- Electrical, internet, rigging, and labor ordering rules
- Move-in and move-out appointment windows
- Advance-warehouse and direct-to-show shipping instructions
- Empty-container storage process
- Flooring requirements
- Accessibility and clear-route expectations

The International Association of Exhibitions and Events publishes [Guidelines for Display Rules and Regulations](https://www.iaee.com/publications/) as a consistency resource for organizers, but the show-specific manual remains the controlling operational document for your booth. Never copy dimensions or mounting assumptions from last year's event without checking the new service kit.

### Mark the usable graphic area

Create a simple booth elevation and plan view. Show the exact frame, drape, table, screen, shelving, product, lighting, and aisle. Mark dimensions in inches, not only feet, and identify anything that can cover the banner:

- A monitor that overlaps the message
- A tall product sample in front of the logo
- A light arm crossing the headline
- Pipe-and-drape folds visible around a narrow banner
- A neighboring booth wall that changes a corner sight line
- Staff standing in the only clear opening

That drawing becomes the source of truth for the banner size and placement.

## 2. Give each banner one job

A booth banner is not a wall-size brochure. From the aisle, it should help the right attendee answer three questions quickly:

1. What company or category is this?
2. Is it relevant to me?
3. Why should I step closer?

Assign one job to every graphic before writing copy.

| Banner position | Primary job | Useful content | Content to move elsewhere |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Back wall | Recognition and relevance from the aisle | Brand, category, one clear buyer benefit | Feature lists, addresses, social handles, legal paragraphs |
| Table front | Confirm identity at conversational distance | Brand or short category line | Detailed product comparison |
| Side rail | Catch traffic approaching from one direction | Short problem or use case | Multiple calls to action |
| Counter panel | Support an active conversation | One proof point or next step | A second headline competing with the back wall |
| Demonstration zone | Explain what the visitor is seeing | Product name and a short result cue | General brand story |

If two banners repeat the same large logo, one of them may be wasting valuable space. If each surface introduces a different slogan, the booth feels fragmented. Build a hierarchy, not a collage.

### Write the aisle message first

Finish this sentence:

> We help **[specific audience]** do **[specific job or outcome]** through **[recognizable category]**.

The banner does not need to print that entire sentence. It needs the few words that make the meaning unmistakable. For example, a fictional packaging-equipment company might lead with its brand and “Packaging Automation,” then use the live demonstration to prove speed or accuracy.

Test the proposed message with someone outside the project. Show it for three seconds and ask what the company offers. If the answer is wrong or vague, fix the hierarchy before polishing the background.

## 3. Measure the display, hardware, and sight line

Measure the physical installation system you will use during the show. Do not select an 8×3 banner because it appears on a popular-size list.

Record:

- Maximum available width and height
- Clear width between uprights
- Pole or crossbar outside diameter
- Table width, height, and visible front drop
- Distance from floor to the planned banner bottom
- Expected closest and farthest viewing distances
- Obstructions in front of the graphic
- Required finish clearance around the artwork
- Packed length of poles, banner, and case

For a six-foot exhibit table, use the [table-banner measuring guide](/blog/banner-size-for-6-foot-table) to decide whether the graphic should cover the full front, leave side margins, or wrap only when the hardware supports it. For a back wall, the [trade show banner size guide](/blog/trade-show-banner-sizes-guide) explains how booth width, neighboring exhibits, and the real viewing distance affect the choice.

### Finished banner size and artwork size are connected

A pole pocket consumes material at an edge. Grommets require clear reinforced space. A hem, fold, or attachment zone can interfere with copy placed too close to the perimeter.

Before artwork begins, ask:

- Is the ordered dimension the final finished size?
- Does the pocket sit inside or add to that size?
- Which edges receive pockets or grommets?
- What clear zone should the designer protect?
- Will a pole or drape cast a shadow across the message?

The [pole-pocket size guide](/blog/banner-pole-pocket-size-guide) explains why the actual pole must be measured and the printer's pocket definition confirmed.

## 4. Choose a material and finish as one system

Banners On The Fly's online vinyl-banner builder currently offers 13oz, 15oz, and 18oz vinyl plus mesh. For a typical indoor exhibit, solid vinyl provides the opaque printed surface most teams expect. Mesh is intended for airflow and is usually relevant to approved outdoor or fence installations, not a standard indoor back wall.

Heavier material is not automatically the best trade-show choice. Think about:

- How the banner will hang
- How often it will be rolled, shipped, and reinstalled
- Whether light or objects behind it could show through
- Packed weight and case size
- Whether the frame can support the finished assembly
- How close visitors will stand
- Whether the venue is indoor, sheltered, or exposed

Use the [13oz, 15oz, and 18oz vinyl comparison](/blog/13oz-vs-15oz-vs-18oz-vinyl-banner-guide) for material tradeoffs, then confirm the live options on the [vinyl-banner page](/vinyl-banners).

### Grommets, pole pockets, or rope?

The finish must match the booth hardware.

| Finish | Useful when | Pre-show question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grommets | The approved frame has distributed attachment points | What cord, bungee, or fastener is allowed, and how will tension be spread? |
| Top and bottom pole pockets | Straight crossbars can support and tension the banner | What are the pole diameters, pocket definition, and usable span? |
| Top pole pocket | The banner is meant to hang from one approved bar | How will the lower edge remain controlled and level? |
| Rope | The installation plan specifically uses rope and the structure permits it | Where will it attach without sagging or blocking the aisle? |

Read the [grommets, pole pockets, and rope guide](/blog/grommets-pole-pockets-hemming-guide) before choosing a finish. Do not order hardware by habit and hope the venue structure matches.

## 5. Build artwork for aisle distance and close inspection

Trade-show graphics work at two distances. From the aisle, the attendee needs brand and category recognition. Inside the booth, the print must still look deliberate at conversational range.

Use a restrained hierarchy:

- Brand name or logo
- Plain-language category
- One buyer-focused benefit or differentiator
- Optional short next step

Keep detailed features, pricing matrices, technical specifications, and multiple URLs in a handout, tablet, product card, or follow-up page. A QR code can support stationary visitors, but it should not become the largest element or replace a readable message. If one is used, follow the [QR-code sizing and scan-test workflow](/blog/qr-code-on-banner-guide).

### Preflight the production file

- Create the document at final size or record the scale clearly.
- Use vector artwork for logos and text when possible.
- Check effective resolution of every raster image at final output size.
- Embed fonts in the PDF.
- Confirm the intended crop in the final export.
- Protect the finish and trim zones.
- Check spelling, model numbers, URLs, and claims.
- Review contrast under lighting similar to the show hall.
- Open the exported production file on a second device.
- Review the printer's full on-screen preview before checkout.

The [print-ready banner artwork guide](/blog/print-ready-banner-artwork-guide) provides the complete file checklist. Do not rely on the editable design file as the final inspection artifact.

## 6. Keep the booth entrance and working area clear

A visually full booth can be operationally unusable. Do not let road cases, banner feet, cords, literature racks, samples, or staff bags narrow the entrance or create a trip hazard.

The Department of Justice's [ADA primer for small businesses](https://www.ada.gov/resources/title-iii-primer/) describes accessible routes as at least three feet wide and warns against blocking them with furniture or display racks. Your exact show, venue, booth type, and installation may have additional requirements, so follow the organizer's accessibility rules and ask when the floor plan is unclear.

During the test setup, check:

- A clear approach from the aisle
- Reach and sight lines from seated and standing positions
- Stable, flush cable protection where permitted
- No pole, foot, or case projecting unexpectedly into circulation
- A conversation position that does not block entry
- Literature and samples reachable without moving the banner
- Storage that keeps boxes and personal items out of the public area

Accessibility is part of the booth plan, not a final walk-through item.

## 7. Separate shipping from material handling

Getting a case to the city is not the same as getting it to the booth. Freeman's [international exhibitor guide](https://www.freeman.com/resources/quick-guide-the-international-exhibitor/) distinguishes carrier shipping from material handling inside the event operation. It also explains the common choice between an advance warehouse and direct-to-show delivery.

Use the show's current service kit to determine:

- Correct recipient and event name
- Booth number
- Advance-warehouse or show-site address
- Accepted delivery dates and hours
- Required labels on every piece
- Carrier appointment or bill-of-lading requirements
- Certified weight or freight documentation
- Who moves the case from receiving to the booth
- Empty-case storage and return process
- Outbound material-handling paperwork

Photograph every packed layer and label. Put a copy of the inventory inside the case and keep another with the booth lead. Do not pack the only tool needed to open the case inside a locked compartment.

## 8. Complete a full test setup

Build the booth exactly as it will stand at the show. A banner held against an office wall does not test the frame, pocket, pole, lighting, table, product, sight line, or packing sequence.

During the test:

1. Time the complete setup with the planned crew.
2. Confirm every frame section and fastener is present.
3. Install the banner using only venue-approved hardware.
4. Level the structure and assess wrinkles or sagging.
5. Place the table, screen, lights, samples, and literature.
6. Photograph the booth from the main aisle approaches.
7. Test the headline at the farthest realistic distance.
8. Walk the entrance and staff positions.
9. Scan any QR code from the printed or full-size proof.
10. Repack everything in the reverse order needed at move-in.

Tag each pole and case with a simple, removable location code. Put small hardware into labeled pouches rather than a loose mixed bag. Record any tool or spare part used during the test so it is not left on the office workbench.

## 9. Pack a banner and booth backup kit

The backup kit should solve ordinary setup failures without creating a rule violation. Confirm every item is permitted by the venue.

### Banner and hardware

- Approved spare straps, cords, or bungees matching the primary system
- Spare fasteners supplied with the display frame
- Compact tape measure
- Small level
- Clean white gloves
- Microfiber cloth
- Lint roller for dark drape
- Hardware pouch and inventory card
- Manufacturer tool for the frame

### Artwork and information

- Print-ready PDF in secure cloud storage
- Lower-resolution reference PDF on a second device
- Logo files and brand-color reference
- Printer order number and contact information
- Approved booth elevation
- Exhibitor manual and service-desk contacts
- Shipping documents and tracking numbers

### Operations

- Phone chargers and an approved power solution
- Lead-capture backup method
- Staff schedule and emergency contacts
- Replenishment list
- Basic first-aid supplies allowed by the venue
- Waste bags and a plan to remove all packing debris

Do not pack unapproved adhesives, knives, ladders, power tools, or improvised rigging because they seem useful. Venue labor and safety rules may restrict who can perform specific work and what equipment can enter the hall.

## 10. Run opening and closing checks every day

### Before the floor opens

- [ ] Banner is level, clean, and evenly tensioned.
- [ ] Headline and category are visible from both primary approaches.
- [ ] Lights are aimed without glare on the print.
- [ ] Entrance and required routes are clear.
- [ ] Cases, cords, bags, and boxes are secured out of sight.
- [ ] Lead capture and QR destinations work.
- [ ] Samples and literature are replenished.
- [ ] Staff can explain the main banner promise in one sentence.
- [ ] A dated opening photo is saved.

### After the floor closes

- [ ] Inspect the banner edges, pockets, grommets, and attachments.
- [ ] Remove dust or marks with the appropriate dry cleaning method.
- [ ] Charge devices and reconcile leads.
- [ ] Replenish materials without blocking the booth.
- [ ] Record damage or missing items immediately.
- [ ] Confirm the next day's access time.
- [ ] Keep the outbound plan and labels accessible.

## Teardown without damaging the banner

Photograph the booth before dismantling so the next setup can be improved. Remove products and loose objects first, then take down the banner without dragging it across the floor or trapping hardware inside the roll.

Make sure the print is clean and dry. Roll it around a wide, rigid core with the printed surface protected; do not sharply fold it to fit a smaller case. The [vinyl banner storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-vinyl-banners) explains how to avoid creases, edge damage, moisture, and pressure during storage.

Count every frame component and tool against the inventory. Label each outbound case before it leaves the booth, and complete the required material-handling documents. Freeman's [first-time exhibitor checklist](https://www.freeman.com/resources/get-trade-show-ready-a-first-time-exhibitor-checklist/) also recommends labeling empty containers before they go to storage and confirming outbound paperwork during move-out.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should be on a trade show booth checklist?

Include the exhibitor manual, booth dimensions, display restrictions, message hierarchy, banner schedule, final artwork, mounting hardware, shipping and material handling, utilities, accessibility, test setup, staff plan, backup kit, daily inspections, and move-out documents. Assign an owner and deadline to every line.

### What should a trade show back-wall banner say?

Lead with the brand, a plain-language category, and one buyer-relevant benefit. The visitor should understand what the company does before entering the booth. Move detailed features, long claims, and multiple calls to action to closer-range materials.

### What size banner works in a 10×10 booth?

There is no universal size. Measure the clear span of the approved frame, height restrictions, neighboring walls, tables, screens, and sight lines. An 8×3 banner can fit some back walls, but that does not make it correct for every 10×10 booth. Order from a dimensioned elevation, not a preset list.

### Are pole pockets or grommets better for a trade show banner?

Use the finish that matches the approved structure. Top-and-bottom pole pockets can create a controlled display on straight crossbars measured for the pockets. Grommets work when the frame provides distributed attachment points and permitted fasteners. Confirm hardware before ordering either finish.

### How far ahead should I order a trade show banner?

Work backward from the event's service-kit deadlines and leave time for message approval, artwork correction, production, carrier transit, inspection, and a full test setup. Do not treat a printer's production window as the entire project schedule; freight and event receiving are separate.

### What should I do when the banner arrives?

Open it promptly in a clean, dry area. Confirm dimensions, artwork, finish, color, edges, and hardware compatibility. Install it on the real frame, photograph the test setup, and report a verified production issue before the freight deadline rather than discovering it at move-in.

## Turn the checklist into a tested booth

Start with the real frame and the show manual, then compare materials and finishing on the [trade-show banner page](/trade-show-banners). When the dimensions and artwork are approved, configure the banner in the [online designer](/design?product=banner), review the full print preview, and order early enough to inspect and test the complete installation before it enters the freight system.

The best booth banner is not the one with the most information. It is the one that fits the structure, survives the trip, communicates from the aisle, and can be installed correctly with the hardware your team actually packed.
