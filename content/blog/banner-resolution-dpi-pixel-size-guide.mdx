# What Resolution Should a Banner Be? A DPI and Pixel-Size Guide

For most banner artwork, the useful question is not simply “Is this file 300 DPI?” It is: **How many pixels will each raster image have at the banner’s final printed size, and how close will people view it?** A photo that is perfectly adequate on an outdoor banner viewed from across a parking lot may look soft on a trade-show display examined from three feet away. A vector logo, meanwhile, can remain sharp at either size because it is not built from a fixed grid of pixels.

As a practical planning range, many large-format banner files use roughly **100–150 pixels per inch at final size** when the finished piece will be viewed from several feet away. Artwork that people will inspect closely may benefit from more resolution, while a very large outdoor piece viewed from much farther away may not need as much. These are planning targets, not a substitute for checking the actual file and the printer’s current specifications.

Adobe’s current [print-resolution guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/ca/photoshop/desktop/crop-resize-transform/resize-adjust-resolution/resolution-specs-for-printing-images.html) explains why 300 PPI is common for smaller prints viewed up close but lower resolutions can work for large-format output viewed from a distance. HP’s [large-format printing guide](https://www.hp.com/us-en/tech-takes/printing/how-to/large-format-printing-best-practices.html) likewise separates image PPI from printer DPI and ties the needed detail to viewing distance.

This guide turns those principles into a repeatable banner-resolution workflow.

## The quick banner-resolution answer

Use the highest-quality original artwork available, then judge every raster element at its **effective PPI at final print size**.

| Typical viewing situation | Practical starting range for raster images at final size | What deserves extra scrutiny |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Close-range counter, booth, or indoor display | 150–200 PPI | Product photography, small supporting copy, fine line art |
| Event, wall, storefront, or general-purpose banner viewed from several feet | 100–150 PPI | Faces, logos supplied as PNGs, QR codes, detailed textures |
| Large outdoor banner viewed mainly from a substantial distance | 75–100 PPI may be workable | Any area people can approach closely; fine detail may still reveal softness |
| True vector text, logos, and shapes | Resolution-independent until rasterized | Effects, shadows, placed photos, and exported raster versions |

Do not interpret the table as a guarantee. Print method, material, image content, contrast, sharpening, compression, enlargement, and actual viewing distance all affect the result. The right response to uncertainty is a file review or physical proof—not inventing pixels the source image never contained.

## PPI and DPI are not the same thing

People commonly say “DPI” when discussing banner files, but two separate measurements are involved:

- **PPI, or pixels per inch, describes the pixel density of a raster image at a particular physical size.** This is the number that tells you whether a photograph or PNG contains enough information for the planned enlargement.
- **DPI, or dots per inch, describes how an output device places ink dots.** Printer resolution is a production characteristic. It does not add real photographic detail to an undersized source image.

For artwork preparation, focus on effective PPI. A printer may place several ink dots to reproduce one image pixel, so printer DPI and image PPI do not map one-to-one.

Adobe’s [image-size documentation](https://helpx.adobe.com/ca/photoshop/desktop/crop-resize-transform/resize-adjust-resolution/resizing-parameters-in-photoshop.html) defines resolution as pixel density and distinguishes physical dimensions from the total pixel dimensions in a file. That distinction is the heart of banner-resolution planning.

## Calculate the pixel dimensions before designing

The formula is straightforward:

**required pixels = final print dimension in inches × target PPI**

Calculate width and height separately. For example, a 3×6-foot banner is 36×72 inches:

- At 100 PPI, the full canvas is **3,600×7,200 pixels**.
- At 150 PPI, it is **5,400×10,800 pixels**.
- At 200 PPI, it is **7,200×14,400 pixels**.

The calculation does not mean every design must be a giant flattened image. A vector-first PDF can keep type, logos, and shapes as vectors while only the placed photographs need enough pixels for their final printed dimensions.

### Pixel-size examples for common banner proportions

| Final banner size | Inches | At 100 PPI | At 150 PPI |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2×4 ft | 24×48 | 2,400×4,800 px | 3,600×7,200 px |
| 3×6 ft | 36×72 | 3,600×7,200 px | 5,400×10,800 px |
| 4×8 ft | 48×96 | 4,800×9,600 px | 7,200×14,400 px |
| 4×10 ft | 48×120 | 4,800×12,000 px | 7,200×18,000 px |

These numbers describe a completely rasterized canvas. A vector file can be much more efficient. The table is most useful when testing a photograph, background texture, or raster logo that will cover most of the banner.

## Effective PPI changes when you resize an image

A file’s metadata can say 300 PPI and still be too small for a banner. The physical size assigned to the image matters.

Suppose a photograph is 3,000 pixels wide:

- At 10 inches wide, it has 300 PPI.
- At 20 inches wide, it has 150 PPI.
- At 30 inches wide, it has 100 PPI.
- At 60 inches wide, it has only 50 PPI.

Nothing happened to the original pixel count. The same pixels were spread across a wider printed area.

Use this formula to test an existing asset:

**effective PPI = pixel dimension ÷ final printed dimension in inches**

Test the width and height. Cropping may make one direction the limiting factor. If a 4,000×3,000-pixel photo is cropped aggressively to a narrow slice, the remaining usable pixels—not the original camera dimensions—determine quality.

## Viewing distance changes what the eye can resolve

A banner is not a business card enlarged to wall size. People usually see large-format work from farther away, so they cannot resolve the same tiny detail they would notice at arm’s length.

Start by identifying the closest normal viewing distance:

- A registration-table banner may be viewed from two or three feet.
- A trade-show back wall may be seen from the aisle and then examined from inside the booth.
- A storefront banner may be read from a sidewalk or moving vehicle.
- A fence banner may be seen primarily from across a field.

Use the closest meaningful viewing condition—not the farthest possible one. If the public can walk directly up to a banner, its photographs and small supporting details need closer inspection even if the main headline is meant for a distant viewer.

Resolution also cannot rescue a poor message hierarchy. The [banner readability guide](/blog/banner-design-readability-tips) explains how viewing distance, letter height, contrast, and message length work together. A razor-sharp paragraph is still the wrong content for a banner if nobody has time to read it.

## Vector artwork solves the right problems

Vector graphics describe shapes mathematically instead of storing a fixed pixel grid. That makes vectors the preferred format for:

- logos;
- large headlines;
- solid-color icons;
- lines, borders, and simple illustrations;
- QR-code modules when the code is generated correctly as vector artwork.

Vectors can scale without becoming blocky. They also tend to produce smaller, more flexible production files than a fully rasterized banner at enormous pixel dimensions.

However, placing a low-resolution JPEG inside an Illustrator document does not convert the photograph into a vector. Nor does saving a raster logo as a PDF magically create vector paths. Inspect the objects inside the file, not merely the filename extension.

Adobe notes that Illustrator effects such as shadows, glows, and some filters are raster effects. Its [Illustrator effects guidance](https://helpx.adobe.com/illustrator/using/effects.html) explains that these effects generate pixels and use the document raster-effects resolution. Review those effects at final output scale even when the surrounding logo and type remain vector.

## Why enlarging a small image does not restore detail

Resampling software can create new pixels, but it must estimate what belongs between the original ones. Upscaling can smooth jagged edges or make a modest enlargement more usable. It cannot recover fine hair, facial detail, fabric texture, or small lettering that was never captured.

Treat AI upscaling and conventional resampling as repair tools, not proof that an image is print-ready. After upscaling:

1. inspect faces, hands, product edges, repeated textures, and small type;
2. compare the new file with the original rather than trusting the new pixel count;
3. check for halos, waxy detail, invented patterns, and oversharpening;
4. view the image at the intended physical size and distance;
5. replace the source if artifacts are visible.

A genuine higher-resolution original, camera export, or vector logo is usually safer than an aggressive enlargement.

## How scaled artwork affects resolution

Large-format designers sometimes work at half, quarter, or tenth scale because full-size artboards can be unwieldy. Scaling is valid only when the printer knows the scale and the effective resolution is calculated correctly.

The relationship is:

**full-size effective PPI = file PPI × artwork scale**

Examples:

- Half-scale artwork at 300 PPI becomes 150 PPI at full size.
- Quarter-scale artwork at 400 PPI becomes 100 PPI at full size.
- Tenth-scale artwork at 300 PPI becomes only 30 PPI at full size.

That final example catches many otherwise careful designers. “300 PPI” sounds reassuring until the 1:10 enlargement is considered.

Always label scaled artwork clearly. Include the intended finished dimensions, and do not expect a production team to infer whether a 7.2-inch-wide file represents 7.2 inches, 72 inches, or another size.

## Check each type of artwork differently

### Photographs

Confirm the cropped pixel dimensions at final placement size. Look for compression blocks, sharpening halos, motion blur, and noise—not just the PPI number. A technically large image can still be visually poor.

### Logos

Request the original vector file when possible. If only a PNG is available, calculate its effective PPI at the intended width and inspect transparent edges for white halos or stair-stepping.

### Type

Keep live type or convert approved final type to vector outlines when the workflow requires it. Do not rely on a screenshot of text. Confirm fonts and spelling before conversion because outlined text is harder to edit.

### QR codes

Keep the code crisp, preserve its quiet zone, and test the actual printed size. Resolution is only one part of reliable scanning; placement, contrast, distortion, and distance also matter. Follow the [banner QR-code testing workflow](/blog/qr-code-on-banner-guide) rather than assuming a code that works on a monitor will work across a venue.

### Shadows and transparency

Inspect raster effects at final size. Fine shadows can band, blur, or show edge boxes after export. Flatten only when the production workflow requires it, and retain an editable master file.

## A banner-resolution preflight workflow

Use this sequence before uploading artwork:

1. **Confirm the finished banner dimensions.** Do not begin resolution math from a guessed preset.
2. **Identify the closest normal viewing distance.** Separate close-range details from the main distance message.
3. **Inventory every raster element.** Include placed photos, PNG logos, textures, shadows, and exported charts.
4. **Calculate effective PPI at final placement size.** Use the cropped dimensions that remain.
5. **Keep vector elements vector.** Avoid flattening type and logos without a production reason.
6. **Inspect at output scale.** Check edges, faces, gradients, transparency, and compression artifacts.
7. **Review color separately.** Resolution and color are different problems; use the [CMYK banner color guide](/blog/color-accuracy-cmyk-banners) for proofing expectations.
8. **Export a production file and reopen it.** Confirm that nothing shifted, disappeared, substituted, or rasterized unexpectedly.
9. **Check the final proportion in the ordering preview.** A sharp file can still be cropped or oriented incorrectly.
10. **Keep the editable source.** The production PDF should not be the only surviving version.

For the broader handoff—including type, safe areas, images, and final review—use the [complete print-ready banner checklist](/blog/print-ready-banner-artwork-guide). If the artwork is being created in Canva, the [Canva banner setup guide](/blog/design-banner-in-canva-for-print) covers proportion and export decisions without pretending the platform can fix an undersized original.

## Common banner-resolution mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Better check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading only the “300 DPI” metadata | Physical enlargement can reduce effective PPI dramatically | Divide actual pixels by final printed inches |
| Converting a JPEG to PDF | The container changes, but the pixel data does not | Inspect the embedded object or obtain vector artwork |
| Judging only from a fit-to-screen preview | The browser shrinks the image and hides defects | Inspect at final scale and intended distance |
| Upscaling until the file has enough pixels | Interpolation may invent detail and artifacts | Compare visually with the original and replace weak sources |
| Building everything as one huge raster image | Files become heavy and type loses vector sharpness | Keep type, logos, and shapes vector where possible |
| Forgetting artwork scale | A 1:10 file expands tenfold and loses nine-tenths of its stated PPI | Multiply file PPI by the scale factor |
| Using the headline’s distance for every element | Close viewers may see soft photos and logos | Plan for the closest normal viewing condition |

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a banner need to be 300 DPI?

Usually not at full printed size. Three hundred PPI is a common target for smaller pieces viewed closely, while large-format banners are often planned at lower effective PPI because they are seen from farther away. Use final dimensions, image content, and closest viewing distance to choose a target.

### Is 72 DPI enough for a banner?

It can be adequate for some large outdoor displays seen from a substantial distance, but it may look soft on indoor, retail, or trade-show graphics that people approach. Calculate effective PPI at final size and inspect the actual image rather than treating 72 as a universal pass or fail.

### How many pixels do I need for a 3×6-foot banner?

A 3×6-foot banner is 36×72 inches. At 100 PPI, that is 3,600×7,200 pixels; at 150 PPI, it is 5,400×10,800 pixels. Those dimensions matter most for a full-banner raster image. Vector-first artwork can keep text and logos sharp without rasterizing the entire canvas.

### Can I use a photo from my phone on a banner?

Possibly. Use the original camera file, not a social-media download or screenshot. Check the cropped pixel dimensions against the final printed size, then inspect noise, focus, compression, and subject detail at the intended viewing distance.

### Is a PDF automatically high resolution?

No. A PDF can contain sharp vectors, low-resolution photographs, or a mixture of both. Inspect the objects inside it. Saving an undersized JPEG inside a PDF does not create additional detail.

### Should I design a banner at full size?

Full size is easiest to reason about when the software supports it. Scaled artwork is also valid when the scale is clearly labeled and every raster element has enough effective PPI after enlargement. Recalculate resolution rather than carrying the file’s displayed PPI over unchanged.

## Build from final dimensions, not a DPI label

The safest workflow is simple: choose the installed size, identify the viewing distance, calculate pixel needs, keep scalable elements vector, and inspect the exported file at its real output scale. That prevents both blurry prints and needlessly oversized files.
